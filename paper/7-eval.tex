\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{subsec:setup}

% VALUE: one setup paragraph: the platform and its power state, the software, the models (one per edge class), the workloads and prompt lengths, the baselines, how TTFT, decode, and turns are measured, the correctness gate, and how the machine state is controlled; defaults stated once
We measure on a GMKtec NucBox EVO-X2 with an AMD Ryzen\texttrademark{} AI Max+ 395 processor, whose XDNA~2 NPU has 8 columns of 4 compute tiles, and 64\,GB of shared system memory.
The host runs Linux~7.0 with the amdxdna driver and XRT~2.25.0~\citep{xdna-driver,xrt-repo}, NPU firmware 1.1.2.65 in its performance power mode, and the CPU's frequency governor and energy preference set to performance.
We evaluate one model per class of edge model: Llama-3.2-1B~\citep{meta2024llama32} and Qwen3-4B~\citep{yang2025qwen3} as dense models of two sizes, Llama-3.1-8B as a larger dense model, Gemma3-4B for attention within a sliding window, and LFM2-1.2B for layers that mix convolution and attention, the last on prefill alone, all with 4-bit quantized weights.
Prompts computed from scratch have 2{,}048 or 4{,}096 tokens, and appends add 512, 1{,}024, or 4{,}096 tokens to a cached context of 4{,}096, 16{,}384, or 28{,}672 tokens, followed by 250 generated tokens in an agent's turn.
These appends range from below the median to beyond the 90th percentile of the new tokens per step in coding-agent traces~\citep{zhu2026tracelab}, whereas the contexts stop at the 32{,}768-token window we build, below the traces' median prefix of 119K tokens, so our results cover agents whose context fits an edge model's window.
The designs are MLIR-AIR's fused prefill and fused decode~\citep{wang2026mlirair}, compiled with MLIR-AIE~\citep{mlir-aie-repo}.
The baselines are each design's own host program on its default split and, as a stronger baseline, the same program after we apply by hand the optimization an expert would reach for first: alternating the segments of attention's two columns so that both start before either is awaited. We construct that arm ourselves; no stack ships it. It uses the same configuration, kernels, and weights as our schedules, and it removes the one false dependence that is visible without reading the command stream, so the gains we report are over a program that has already had the obvious fix; \E{FastFlowLM~\citep{fastflowlm-repo} on the same weights gives an absolute reference.}
Every run's logits must be bit-identical to the baseline's, every append's logits bit-identical to those of a full prefill of the whole prompt, and the kernel log must show no driver or IOMMU message during the run.
These gates compare a schedule with the stack's own output rather than with an independent implementation of the model, and where a stack also supplies a floating-point forward we compare against that.
On Llama-3.2-1B, which carries our decode and turn results, the prefill's keys and values differ from that forward by up to 3.0\% and 7.5\% across layers, yet decode seeded from the forward's own keys and values, from one split's, and from the other's emits the same seventeen tokens.
On Gemma3-4B the same comparison diverges at the fifth decoded token and we report no turn for that model; the difference is the shipped program's own, since a build made by the module's own builder and loaded by the module's own path produces, bit for bit, the keys and values our harness hands to decode.
Appendix~E gives both comparisons and the per-layer differences. We still report that model's times, because under the static control of Section~\ref{subsec:model} a dispatch's work is fixed by its runtime parameters rather than by the values it computes, so the schedule's duration does not depend on the arithmetic the shipped program produces; what the comparison bears on is that program's accuracy, not our measurement of it.

The two grids are disjoint, and no turn cell carries a scored prediction.
TTFT is the wall time of the prefill call, from token identifiers to logits, decode time per token is measured over 250 greedy tokens after a prefill of the context, and a turn's time is its append and its 250 decoded tokens. Everywhere else in this section the composed arm differs from its baseline in three ways at once: issue is reordered across column groups, buffers are renamed into rings, and the split is chosen per regime.

\subsection{Synthetic Designs}
\label{subsec:synthetic}
% VALUE: three hand-written designs (not rewrites) test the all-or-nothing finding directly and fill the grid's two empty cells; each carries a certificate registered before it ran, that a removal off the binding cycle is worth nothing, and one certificate misses on the slow side at one work level, which is reported with its size
Three hand-written designs, which are not rewrites of shipped designs, test the finding of Section~\ref{sec:intro} and the two cells of Table~\ref{tab:grid} that hold no instance in the designs we rewrite.
Each registered, before it ran, that a removal our analysis places off the binding cycle is worth $1.00 \pm 0.01$.

The first puts one compute tile in a 2{,}048-iteration loop whose input and output each pass through an object FIFO, so that a FIFO of depth one closes a write-after-read cycle through the core and the two such edges lie on the same cycle.
At three levels of core work, no single removal gained more than 1.0\% and none changed the period by more than 1.8\% in either direction, the six measuring 1.010, 1.003, 1.000, 0.998, 0.982, and 0.988. Removing both gains $1.60\times$ where the core's work is smallest, $1.15\times$ where it is near the transfer, and $1.03\times$ where it exceeds it. The largest of those three rests on the least stable measurement in this design: at that level the depth-one baseline's repetitions span 193\% of their median, where every other spread here is between 0.4\% and 4.9\%, so we read the ordering across the three levels rather than the size of the first.
The registered band held at two of the three levels and missed at the third, where both single removals came out marginally slower rather than unchanged, by 0.008 and 0.002.
The pair beat the better single by more than the band at every level.

The other two designs fill the empty cells, so neither is empty by construction.
For a name dependence in the CP's stream, two operators on separate columns are issued over eight chunks with one shared buffer, which makes each chunk's producer wait for the previous chunk's consumer; a two-entry ring removes that wait and gains 1.3\%, which clears its registered threshold by 0.003 but is smaller than the 1.5--2.0\% spread between repetitions, so it demonstrates the class rather than a gain worth taking.
For an order dependence inside a loop, a consumer holds its first acquire across a pair of elements, which is the hand edit of Section~\ref{subsec:ablation}; at buffer depth two that edge closes the producer--consumer cycle and removing it gains $2.00\times$, and at depth four the same removal is worth 0.999, because what binds there is each core's own per-element loop rather than the edge. That loop compiles to 38 instruction bundles per four elements at depth four against 12 at depth two, since the compiler unrolls it at both depths but software-pipelines it only at depth two, and the ratio of those counts accounts for the measured slowdown to within 2\%. The registration had assumed that the producer's and consumer's own times do not depend on the buffer depth; that assumption failed, and code generation is why.
The handoff the turn metric excludes is not small, and both arms pay it: in its clean state it costs 399--949\,ms on Llama-3.2-1B, 6.2--8.6\% of the turn, and 619--2{,}550\,ms on Qwen3-4B, 3.4--8.2\%.
On one Qwen3-4B cell it reached 53.6\,s, more than half the turn, which is the host state Section~\ref{subsec:limits} describes and the reason we price it apart from the schedules rather than inside them.

Against the faster of the two shipped splits, the composed schedule shortens a turn by $1.06$--$1.23\times$ at a 4{,}096-token cached context, $1.15$--$1.32\times$ at 16{,}384, and $1.33\times$ at 28{,}672 on the model we measure there, so the gain rises with the cached context on both models and with the size of the step.
At the step size the traces make most common, roughly a thousand new tokens onto a 16{,}384-token context, a turn is $1.15$--$1.26\times$ shorter, and $1.28$--$1.50\times$ shorter than the shipped program on its default split.
Decode carries most of this, and the clearest sign of that is where prefill loses: at 512-token appends the composed prefill runs at $0.88$--$0.93\times$ of the existing order, yet the turn is still $1.06$--$1.26\times$ shorter, because the generated tokens dominate it.
That is the regime Section~\ref{subsec:removal} names, in which removing a dependence does not pay on its own phase and the schedule is still worth composing end to end.

% VALUE: which group binds the bound flips with the workload, and that flip is what moves the faster split; the overlap cost of the bound appears only where the GEMMs bind; the rest of the distance to the bound is host work the schedule does not hide
Which group binds the lower bound flips with the workload.
From scratch at 2{,}048 tokens the GEMMs bind at every split on both models, whereas on an append to a 16{,}384-token context attention binds at every split.
The split names the number of attention columns, so a GEMM-bound configuration is served by fewer of them and an attention-bound one by more, which is why the faster split moves from two columns for prompts computed from scratch to four for appends.
The overlap cost of Section~\ref{subsec:removal} appears only where the GEMMs bind, at 1.0--5.1\% of TTFT, and accounts for part of the distance to the bound.
The remainder is the CP's time and the host work that the schedule does not hide.
On Llama-3.1-8B at 4{,}096 tokens, the composed prefill reads 51.5~GB/s averaged over its whole time including host work, which is 93--96\% of the cap, so on that model the schedule leaves little for any further reordering to win.

We formulated the bound after writing the passes, so the bounds reported here were computed before each gain was measured rather than before the passes were built.
Reading the same cells as a gap rather than a ratio says how much of what is available we take.
Against the shipped program at its own faster split, and with the bound at the best split as the floor, the composed schedule closes a median 65\% of the distance between them, and between 34\% and 76\% across the twelve cells.
