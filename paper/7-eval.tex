\subsection{Where Rescheduling Pays}
\label{subsec:pays}
\label{subsec:clause}

% VALUE: the condition of Section 3.3, stated in full and tested: what rescheduling recovers is set by how a design cuts its work and by the prompt length; four tests, in the claim's order: exposure and bounds per design class across the census (the cut), the shape across prompt lengths (the length), a synthetic design that varies the balance and the chunk count directly, and one control per clause
What rescheduling recovers is set by how a design cuts its work and by the prompt length, as Section~\ref{subsec:question} states, and we test that claim across the design classes of our census, across prompt lengths, on a synthetic design, and with one control per clause.

\label{subsec:exposure}
% VALUE: what the exposure measures and what it does not: per workload, the exposure M (share of wall time in commands whose configuration holds two or more operators), the bound b on reorders inside those commands, and the ceiling they imply; these cover the reorders inside a design's existing commands, which is all a closed stack lets us rewrite, whereas on an open fused design that issues one operator per command the composer re-cuts the commands, which the per-removal bound prices
How a design cuts its work into configurations, commands, and buffers sets how much of its work meets the condition, and so what rescheduling can recover.
We state for each design its \emph{exposure} $M$, the share of the wall time spent in commands whose configuration holds two or more operators, taken per recorded prompt on a closed stack.
A closed stack's rewrites stay inside its existing commands, apart from reorders and merges of commands that no host step separates and narrowings of waits whose host accesses are observed (Section~\ref{subsec:closed}).
For such a stack we also state $b$, the joint bound of Section~\ref{subsec:removal} over every legal rewrite inside those commands.
The two imply a ceiling on the end-to-end gain from inside commands of $1 / \bigl((1 - M) + M / b\bigr)$, which separates a design that exposes little work from one whose exposed work cannot overlap.
An open fused design leaves more room, since OPCORD's composer also re-cuts its commands, so that operators issued one per command come to share commands, a rewrite that the bound of each false order across commands covers.
On MLIR-AIR's prefill, which spends nearly all of its time on one configuration that holds every operator and so has an exposure near one, this re-cut carries nearly all of the gain at the splits OPCORD chooses (Section~\ref{subsec:ablation}), because each of its commands issues one operator and leaves room inside only for the stream pass's alternation within attention.
% SOURCE: exposure metric and ceiling (aie-cosched registered 2a7990a; script 3d11670; rows a3ef131; partb/README).

\label{subsec:observed}
% VALUE: the census by design class, each placed by its cuts, with what the condition predicts and what the bounds and measurements show: MLIR-AIR's prefill (fused configuration, one operator per command) gains, as Section 6.2 measures; FastFlowLM's flash prefill (fused configuration, one column group per command, the same shape) has M 0.82-0.87 but runs each layer's attention once over the whole prompt (no chunks; a 1,024-token cap), so every GEMM feeds or reads it and every turn between groups is a true dependence, failing the second clause at every length it accepts; b 1.000 inside its one-group commands; its one rewrite worth measuring (a merge at the shortest prompts) is the bound's check in Section 6.6; its decode pairs share an engine; IRON's modular operator sequences share no command and their streams price at one; the non-LLM designs overlap through dataflow; so every design the condition says can gain is one whose gain we measure
Across the 112 designs of our census (Table~\ref{tab:census}), the condition of Section~\ref{subsec:question}, read from each design's artifacts, predicts gains only where a false order in a fused configuration holds back work on core tiles and DMA channels that the work it waits on does not use, resources a rewrite cannot duplicate, with no true dependence between.
Measurement bears this out for MLIR-AIR's prefill and decode, and the bounds of Section~\ref{subsec:removal}, computed with measured durations and reported below, show that the designs where the condition predicts no gain have little to recover.
MLIR-AIR's chunked fused prefill holds every operator in one configuration but issues one command per operator and chunk, with attention and the GEMMs on disjoint column groups, so its column groups take turns on work that meets both clauses (Section~\ref{subsec:losses}), and the gains of Section~\ref{subsec:e2e} are measured there.
MLIR-AIR's fused decode keeps one buffer per role in its attention loop, so each block's transfer waits on the previous block's compute, and the loop pass's gain of Section~\ref{subsec:e2e} is measured there, whereas renaming only the keys and values, which leaves the score buffer closing the cycle, gains 0.3\% (Section~\ref{subsec:ablation}).

FastFlowLM's prefill exposes most of its time, yet leaves almost no overlap to recover, because every turn between its groups is a true dependence.
Its flash prefill, taken as released, accepts prompts of up to 1{,}024 tokens, its single-turn context, while FastFlowLM serves longer prompts with a separate engine that the user chooses by model tag, not by prompt length.

The flash prefill spends most of its time on one configuration that holds the operators of both column groups, for an exposure $M$ of 0.82--0.87, and issues each command to one group, so its groups take turns across commands as MLIR-AIR's do.
The two differ in how they cut the prompt into commands: MLIR-AIR's reimplementation runs it in 128-token chunks, so the GEMMs of later chunks are free to run beside one chunk's attention (Section~\ref{subsec:losses}), whereas FastFlowLM runs each layer's attention once over the whole prompt, so every GEMM command of a layer either feeds that attention or reads its output.
Each turn therefore fails the condition's second clause at every length the design accepts: from the GEMMs to attention its host reads a command's output before it issues the next, and from attention to the GEMMs the host's wait is false, but the next command's read of attention's output in device memory orders the same turn.
The false orders inside its commands bound at $b = 1.000\times$, a ceiling of $1\times$ however large $M$ is.
The joint bound over the whole legal rewrite space of Section~\ref{subsec:closed}, across commands as well as inside them, its host's false waits after attention included, whose removal could only let host steps start earlier, stays below $1.05\times$ with measured durations at every prompt from 128 to 1{,}024 tokens, apart from the merge of two commands at the shortest prompts, which saves a dispatch rather than freeing overlap (Section~\ref{subsec:census}).
Its decode runs one command per step, whose false orders all hold apart work that shares a core tile or a DMA channel, and bounds at $1.000$--$1.009\times$.

IRON's modular operator sequences give each operator its own configuration of the whole array, so consecutive operators share every core tile and DMA channel, which the first clause excludes, their operators share no command, their exposure is zero, and they pay reconfiguration between operators instead, and their 32 streams, which issue their transfers ahead of their waits, all bound at $1.000\times$.
Each of the non-LLM designs we read runs as one command whose stages already overlap through the array's own dataflow, and the 3 that IRON builds also bound at $1.000\times$.
Every overlap gain we measure therefore falls where the condition says one can arise, and where it says none can, the bounds, computed with measured durations, stay at or near $1\times$, while the controls below test each clause directly.

The prompt length sets the balance of the work the two groups can overlap, since in a prefill cut into chunks of 128 tokens attention's work per chunk grows with the chunk's position while the GEMMs' work per chunk stays fixed, so overlapping the two column groups across chunks hides the shorter group's time behind the longer one's.
At one split on every LLM, the reorder ties at one chunk and loses at two, because the per-command costs and the time to fill and drain the pipeline of commands outweigh the overlap, and it gains from four chunks, rising to about $2\times$ at 8--16K tokens where the groups' work balances, before it falls at 32K, where attention outweighs the GEMMs.
The balance alone accounts for this shape from 1K to 4K tokens, where an estimate from the two groups' busy times and the host time the overlap hides, a shape estimate rather than a bound, tracks the gain within 5\%, but only the event model, which composes each command, prices the dispatch costs at few chunks and the incomplete overlap at long balanced prompts.
The split moves this balance, so the split that minimizes TTFT also changes with the prompt length.
