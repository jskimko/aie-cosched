Although we evaluate one NPU, our findings rest on properties that other spatial accelerators may share.
Where an in-order issuer mediates the concurrency of an array, its instruction order may act as the schedule, so packing work into fewer dispatches may yield little concurrency, and a second queue or a completion signal per group of columns could remove the rationing in hardware (Sections~\ref{subsec:semantics} and~\ref{subsec:ablation}).
Where operators are partitioned spatially, the faster partition may change between computing a prompt from scratch and appending to a cached context, so an evaluation of prompts computed from scratch alone could choose the partition that is slower for agents (Section~\ref{subsec:ablation}).
Where issue returns at once and waits block, probes of each operator alone may rank configurations reliably even where they misprice them, so a configuration can be chosen before it runs (Section~\ref{subsec:accuracy}).
Once the issue order is right, the binding term may move from the issuer to the groups' own busy time, so further gains could come from kernels and from the balance of work between groups rather than from scheduling (Section~\ref{subsec:bound-eval}).
Where an operator reuses one buffer across the iterations of a loop, reordering issue across operators cannot reach the resulting wait, whereas renaming the buffer inside the operator, as rings do across operators, could (Section~\ref{subsec:decode-eval}).
Finally, where agents append to long contexts, prefill becomes bound by attention and decode by the KV cache, so gains measured on prompts computed from scratch may misstate what an agent's turn gains (Section~\ref{subsec:turns}).

\paragraph{Limits.}
\label{subsec:limits}
% VALUE: the limits a reader should hold us to, each tied to what we did or did not establish: one array generation, reconfiguration held fixed, the production engine as a reference point whose difference is attributed by kind at one length, a host memory state we do not control on the handoff, two registered predictions that failed, and the cases the analysis declines
We measure one generation of one vendor's array, so the transfer above rests on the properties we name rather than on measurements elsewhere.
Our passes reorder and rename within one configuration of the array and leave every reconfiguration point where the stack put it, so the gains we report hold with those points unchanged and say nothing about moving them.
The production engine is a reference point measured through its own server rather than a controlled comparison. At the one prompt length where we record both stacks the same way, the whole of the difference is accounted for by kind, and its largest part is host time with no command on the device, which we can place but not decompose; Appendix~B gives the split and the lengths it does not cover.7--12.7$\times$ its clean curve while the kernel reported huge-page fault fallbacks, compaction stalls, and no free high-order blocks, and then returned to the clean curve minutes later without intervention.
We therefore measure the handoff in every repetition rather than pricing it from a fit, and record those counters with each turn, but whether the handoffs recorded earlier were in that state is not established, because those runs predate the counters.
Producing a schedule is compile-time work and sits outside the times we report, as the shipped program's own compilation does; charged on every request instead of once per shape it would still leave the composed schedule ahead at every cell we measure (Section~\ref{subsec:setup}).
Our gates establish that a rewrite preserves the stack's own output, not that the stack reproduces a reference implementation, and one configuration we examined does not: Section~\ref{subsec:setup} reports it and we measure no turn there.
Of the predictions we registered before the runs, two failed: the end-to-end gain left its band on half its cells, and the distance to the bound left its range on five of twelve, both reported in Section~\ref{sec:eval} as they came out.
Finally, the analysis declines what it cannot classify rather than guessing, so a loop whose body holds nested control is reported as unsupported, which in the decode design covers every loop outside attention.

% VALUE: who owns concurrency: GPUs once lost concurrency to false dependences among streams sharing one hardware work queue, which hardware queues removed; on XDNA 2 one in-order CP per context leaves that job to the program, so the compiler owns concurrency across column groups, which places XDNA 2 between GPUs that decide overlap at run time and statically timed machines; the device reads busy while one group works at a time
GPUs once lost concurrency in the same way, where streams that shared one hardware work queue could wait on each other, and NVIDIA's Kepler GPUs added hardware queues that remove these false dependences~\citep{nvidia-kepler-tuning}.
On XDNA~2, one in-order CP per hardware context leaves this job to the program, so the compiler owns concurrency across column groups, much as Rammer's compile-time schedules do on GPUs and IPUs~\citep{ma2020rammer}.
This places XDNA~2 between GPUs, whose hardware decides overlap at run time, and statically timed machines such as the TSP, whose compiler fixes when every instruction executes~\citep{abts2020tsp}, because its program fixes the order of each engine's work but not its duration.
A device that reads busy for 92--97\% of TTFT while only one column group works at a time shows why this ownership matters, since utilization measured per device hides the idle columns (Section~\ref{subsec:losses}).
Other arrays whose engines run their programs in order, such as other AIE generations, may share this property, so the same analysis could apply where their streams can be read.
